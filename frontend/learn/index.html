Every module in the Solver applies a method you can follow by hand. This is what each one does, when it applies, and what to check when the answer looks wrong.

Transforms

Laplace

The Laplace transform turns differentiation into multiplication, which is what makes it useful for linear differential equations with constant coefficients: an equation in t becomes an algebraic equation in s, you solve the algebra, and you invert.

The shifting theorems do most of the work in practice. The first shifting theorem handles e^(at) f(t) by replacing s with s - a; the second handles a delayed function. If a transform looks unfamiliar, it is usually a standard one with a shift applied. Try it in the Solver →

Fourier

Where Laplace suits initial-value problems, the Fourier transform suits signals defined over all of x. The sine and cosine transforms are the odd and even halves of it, and are the right choice on a half-range.

Z

The discrete counterpart: sequences rather than functions, difference equations rather than differential ones. The inverse is where most errors happen, because a repeated pole changes the form of the answer — a pole of multiplicity m contributes a binomial factor, and getting that factor wrong produces a sequence that is right at n = 0 and wrong everywhere after. Try it in the Solver →

Ordinary differential equations

For a linear equation with constant coefficients the solution splits in two. The complementary function comes from the auxiliary equation’s roots: distinct real roots give exponentials, a repeated root multiplies by x, and a complex pair gives a sine and cosine.

The particular integral depends on the forcing term. The one case worth watching is resonance — when the forcing matches a root of the auxiliary equation, the ordinary formula divides by zero and the answer picks up a factor of x. MathNova reports resonance and its multiplicity explicitly, because it is the most common place a hand calculation goes wrong. Try it in the Solver →

First-order equations

Four methods cover most of a syllabus: separate the variables when it separates; use an integrating factor when it is linear; substitute v = y^(1-n) for Bernoulli; and check exactness before anything else, because an exact equation is the quickest of the four when it applies.

Partial differential equations

Forming a PDE means eliminating the arbitrary constants from a relation: differentiate with respect to each variable, then eliminate. Lagrange’s linear equation Pp + Qq = R is solved through the auxiliary system dx/P = dy/Q = dz/R, giving two independent integrals whose arbitrary function is the general solution. Numerical methods

Numerical methods trade exactness for reach: they answer questions that have no closed form. The cost is that every answer carries an error, and the method you choose decides how that error behaves.

  • Bisection always converges if the bracket is valid, but slowly — one bit per step.
  • Newton–Raphson converges quadratically when it converges at all, and diverges readily from a poor starting point or a near-zero derivative.
  • Secant avoids needing the derivative, at a slightly slower rate than Newton.
  • Simpson’s rules integrate exactly up to cubics, so they beat the trapezoidal rule on smooth integrands — but 1/3 needs an even number of intervals and 3/8 a multiple of three.

Try them in the Solver →

Linear algebra

Eigenvalues come from the characteristic polynomial, and for anything past 3×3 the exact roots stop being expressible in a useful closed form. MathNova keeps results exact where exact form exists and is explicit when it does not, rather than silently switching to decimals. Try it in the Solver →

Fourier series

A periodic function becomes a sum of harmonics. The coefficients are integrals over one period, and the partial sum shows how quickly the series converges — slowly near a jump, where the overshoot at a discontinuity never quite disappears no matter how many terms you take. Try it in the Solver →
